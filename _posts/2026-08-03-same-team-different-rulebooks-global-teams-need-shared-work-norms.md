Your global team may not have a communication problem. It may have an invisible-rulebook problem.

A leader sees late updates, quiet meetings, missed signals, or hesitation around decisions and starts to wonder: Why do people not speak up? Why did they not tell me sooner?

Sometimes the issue is performance. But often, across cultures and geographies, people work from different assumptions about what good work looks like.

Most leaders do not assume their way is the only right way. They work from what feels normal: this is how I communicate, show initiative, give feedback, and act professionally. Others may interpret behaviors differently.

Research on global virtual teams shows this tension clearly. Cultural diversity can improve decision-making, but it can also create communication challenges when teams do not intentionally manage how people share information and interpret meaning. [Shachaf, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2007.12.003).

For one person, respect means speaking directly. For another, respect means not contradicting a senior person in public. For one person, ownership means acting independently. For another, ownership means checking alignment first.

When expectations stay implicit, difference can get misread as deficiency. Someone careful gets labeled passive. Someone direct gets called difficult.

Trust and knowledge sharing are central to global team effectiveness. Pinjani and Palvia found that both help explain how diversity connects to effectiveness in global virtual teams. [Pinjani & Palvia, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2012.10.002).

So what can leaders do?

Make the invisible visible. Define what good means:

- What does a useful update include?
- When should risks be escalated?
- How do we disagree respectfully?
- How do we confirm understanding after meetings?

This builds shared mental models: common understandings of goals, roles, tasks, and how the team works together. A meta-analysis found that team interventions can improve them and support more effective teamwork. [Lines et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000288).

Check whose norms are being rewarded. Who speaks most easily in meetings? Whose style gets seen as leadership presence? Who adapts most to be understood?

## Key Takeaway for Leaders

- Do not assume everyone shares the same rulebook.
- Define expectations before judging behavior.
- Use written follow-up to clarify decisions, owners, deadlines, and risks.
- Build team norms that do not simply default to the leader's personal or cultural style.

Leadership across cultures is not about making everyone adapt to your default. It is about creating a shared way of working so people do not have to guess what good looks like.
