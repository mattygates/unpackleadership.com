A common frustration leaders share is some version of this:

*I gave the feedback clearly. I tried to be fair. I was specific about what needed to change. But the person shut down, got defensive, nodded along and changed nothing, or later said I was being too negative.*

That's a hard place to be as a leader.

You're trying to help someone improve, but the conversation creates distance instead. You're trying to build accountability, but the feedback doesn't turn into action. At some point, you may start wondering: *Am I being too direct? Too vague? Too soft? Is this a performance issue, a trust issue, or a communication issue?*

In multicultural teams, the answer may be: it depends.

Feedback is supposed to help people improve. But it doesn't land in a vacuum. It lands inside a relationship, a power dynamic, a team culture, and sometimes a set of cultural assumptions people may not realize they're carrying.

Imagine a manager saying, "I'd like you to be more direct in meetings."

To one person, that may sound like useful coaching. To another, it may sound like criticism of their personality. Someone from a culture where disagreement is expected may hear it as a simple adjustment. Someone from a culture where harmony, hierarchy, or saving face matters more may hear it as a warning that they've failed.

No one is necessarily wrong.

They may just be interpreting the same message through different assumptions.

> Feedback doesn't land only because it's clear. It lands when there's enough trust, context, and shared meaning for the person to use it.

That's why feedback in diverse teams requires more than a good script.

Leaders often focus on delivery: be specific, be timely, be kind, be direct. Those things matter. But research on team feedback suggests that culture can shape how feedback is perceived, processed, and acted on. A message that feels efficient to one person may feel abrupt to another. A conversation intended as coaching may feel like public embarrassment, loss of face, or a sign that the relationship has been damaged.

This doesn't mean leaders should avoid feedback. It means they need to understand what the feedback moment requires.

Some people expect direct feedback and see indirect language as vague. Others expect feedback to be softened, private, or connected to the relationship first. Some employees are comfortable asking clarifying questions. Others may come from environments where hierarchy makes it harder to say, "I don't understand what you want me to do differently."

That's where leaders can misread silence.

A quiet employee may not be disengaged. They may be showing respect. They may be processing. They may be unsure whether it's safe to respond honestly.

Research on multicultural teams points to the importance of trust, transparency, inclusive leadership, and open feedback channels. Feedback works better when people believe the leader is trying to help them succeed, not simply pointing out what's wrong. It also works better when expectations are explicit and the next step is clear.

For leaders, the practical shift is this: don't only ask, "Was my feedback clear?"

Ask, "Was it usable?"

Usable feedback gives the person enough context to understand the concern, enough safety to ask questions, and enough direction to know what to do next.

A leader doesn't need to become an expert in every culture represented on the team. But they do need cultural curiosity. They need to notice when their default communication style may not be the only reasonable one.

Because when feedback doesn't land, the problem may not be effort, attitude, or accountability. Sometimes the leader and employee are working from different assumptions about what feedback is supposed to sound like, what it means, and how a person is expected to respond.

The goal isn't to make feedback softer.

The goal is to make it clearer, more respectful, and more useful across differences.

## What Leaders Can Pay Attention To

- **Notice how people respond to feedback.** Silence, defensiveness, over-apologizing, or quick agreement can all mean different things. Don't assume you know what the response means without checking.

- **Make the purpose explicit.** Say whether the conversation is coaching, correction, development, or a performance concern. People use feedback better when they understand what kind of conversation they're in.

- **Separate directness from usefulness.** Direct feedback isn't automatically better feedback. Ask whether the person knows exactly what to keep doing, stop doing, or adjust.

- **Create room for clarification.** Instead of ending with "Does that make sense?" ask, "What part of this feels clear, and what part should we talk through?" This makes questions easier to ask.

- **Be careful with public feedback.** In some teams, quick feedback in front of others may feel normal. In others, it may create embarrassment or loss of trust. When in doubt, use private feedback for anything sensitive.
