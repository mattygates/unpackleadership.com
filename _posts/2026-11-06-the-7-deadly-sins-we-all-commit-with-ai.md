I do my best thinking on a treadmill, usually with a show on. Lately that show has been *Dragon's Dogma*: seven episodes, each named for a deadly sin, about a man whose hunt for a dragon slowly costs him his humanity.

By the final episode, I had stopped thinking about the dragon. I was thinking about how I use AI.

**TL;DR:** AI doesn't create new bad habits. It removes the friction that slowed them down. The sharpest test for leaders is the performance review: if AI writes it only to tick a box, the person it's about gets nothing.

---

## Gluttony: taking in without digesting

When ChatGPT first came out, I asked it to summarize books and walked away feeling like I'd read them. Psychologists have found that searching the internet for an explanation makes people rate their own knowledge higher, even when the search turns up nothing. The mind blurs "I can find it" into "I know it," and a tidy summary blurs it further.

The issue isn't how much you take in. It's how much you could explain tomorrow.

## Pride: "I've read it, so I know it"

Pride is what the feeling of knowing does next. I'd even tell people I'd read the book. Then someone would ask a question that went past the summary, and I'd find I had only bullet points.

AI makes this easier, because its answers arrive fluent and confident. In one survey, 32.8% of executives said they trust AI over their own judgment, versus 16.3% of individual contributors. It can't tell us why. One possibility: people with authority hear "no" less often, and a polished answer never says it.

## Sloth: skipping the work

I use AI regularly, and I feel the pull to hand over the thinking. A workshop outline can look polished, but I still have to understand why each activity belongs and draw out the experience already in the room. The outline can't do that.

The cost can be steep. Deloitte refunded part of a A$440,000 government contract after its report was found to contain citations that didn't exist, including a quote attributed to a federal judge. An outside researcher spotted them. Research from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon suggests why this happens: people more confident in AI thought less critically, while people more confident in their own skills checked more. Checking takes a skill you keep practicing.

## When the review is just a box to tick

Now picture a Friday afternoon. Eight performance reviews are due, the notes are thin, and the system keeps sending reminders. Six in ten managers in one survey said they rely on AI to make decisions about their direct reports, and two-thirds of those using it had received no formal training. It's tempting to paste the notes in and get back something fluent and professional in seconds.

The box gets ticked. The system is satisfied. Nobody grows.

> A review written to be finished is not the same as a review written to help someone grow.

AI writes what could be said about anyone: "strong collaborator," "room to grow in communication." People notice. In a study of about 1,100 professionals, even a congratulatory message written with heavy AI help made supervisors look less sincere: only about half of employees saw them that way, versus 83% when the AI assistance was light. Reviews struggled before AI, too: Gallup found only 14% of employees strongly agree theirs inspire them to improve.

Try this test: could this paragraph describe anyone on your team? If so, it isn't feedback yet. And when the employee asks, "What makes you say that?" can you answer?

## Four more sins, one pattern

Envy is adopting a tool because someone else has it. Lust is falling for a launch instead of solving a problem. Greed is taking more than you give. Wrath is turning on anyone who questions your AI use.

Each has a matching virtue, and I've laid out all seven in [a carousel on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mattygates/). ## Where to start

Start with four habits.

**Write the first draft yourself.** Use AI afterward to tighten it, not to start it.

**Bring an example for every claim.** If you can't name a moment you saw, cut the line. For more on feedback that stays specific and accountable, see [Nice Isn't Weak: Give Feedback Without Losing Accountability](/nice-isnt-weak-give-feedback-without-losing-accountability/).

**Ask before you write.** "Where do you want to be stronger in six months?" gives the review a purpose beyond the form.

**Say where AI helped.** Being upfront is easier than being found out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it wrong to use AI for reviews at all?**

No. It's useful for organizing a year of your own notes, spotting patterns, and tightening wording. It can't tell you what happened. AI can help you say it, but only you can supply what you saw.

**What if I don't have good notes, or the time?**

That's a signal to change the routine, not to outsource the judgment. Keep a running log: two lines after each meaningful moment. Gallup recommends holding progress conversations more often, at least every six months, which also leaves you with something to draw on when the review comes due. For how to keep those conversations forward-looking, see [Your Feedback Is Not Failing Because You Are Too Nice](/your-feedback-is-not-failing-because-you-are-too-nice/).

**What if my team uses AI for their self-reviews?**

Expect it, and treat it like any other draft. Ask for the example behind each claim. The question that keeps your feedback honest keeps theirs honest, too: what makes you say that?
