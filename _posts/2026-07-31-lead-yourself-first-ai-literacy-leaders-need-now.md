AI is showing up in more leadership work every week: drafting communications, summarizing information, generating options, and helping teams move faster.

That can create a quiet pressure for leaders. Some feel they need to become AI experts overnight. Others avoid the tools altogether, worried about mistakes, bias, privacy, or simply getting it wrong.

Neither response is especially helpful.

The more useful goal is AI literacy: enough understanding to know what a tool can do, what it cannot do, and when your own judgment still needs to lead.

Imagine a manager preparing for a difficult team conversation. They ask AI to draft a message. The draft is clear, polished, and completely misses the history, emotion, and relationships involved. A leader with AI literacy does not simply send it. They question it, add the missing context, check the assumptions, and remain accountable for the outcome.

> AI literacy is not about having all the answers. It is about knowing which questions to ask before you act.

Research supports that distinction. AI literacy includes understanding how AI works at a practical level, using it appropriately, evaluating its outputs, and recognizing ethical considerations. It is not just prompt-writing. A study of top management teams also found that stronger AI literacy was associated with greater organizational readiness and ability to implement AI.

For leaders, this is a Lead Self practice. Stay curious. Try the tools. Learn their limits. Notice when an answer sounds confident but needs checking. Most importantly, do not outsource your judgment.

AI can help leaders work differently. It cannot replace the responsibility to lead wisely.
